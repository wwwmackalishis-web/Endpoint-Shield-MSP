# Endpoint Shield Solutions — Sales Deck Outline

**Audience:** Medical and dental office managers
**Length:** 5 slides, 12–15 minutes, leaving 15 for questions
**Objective:** Move from "we already have an IT guy" to a scheduled risk assessment

**Framing principle:** An office manager does not buy antivirus. They buy the removal of a personal liability they think about at 2 a.m. and cannot currently see. Every slide should answer *"what am I exposed to, and how would I know?"* — never *"here is what our software does."*

---

## Slide 1 — The Exposure Nobody Owns

**Headline:** *Your practice is a covered entity. Your workstations are the gap.*

**On the slide**

- The HHS Office for Civil Rights publishes every breach affecting 500+ individuals on a public portal — the "Wall of Shame." Small practices appear on it regularly.
- OCR civil monetary penalties are tiered by culpability, up to a statutory annual cap per violation category. Penalties for "willful neglect — not corrected" sit at the top tier.
- Under the HITECH Act, a Business Associate carries **direct** liability. Your IT vendor's exposure does not replace yours.
- The most common finding in OCR enforcement actions against small providers is not exotic: it is the absence of a documented, current risk analysis.

**What you say**

"You've done the training. You've got the policies binder. Here's the question I'd ask if I were the auditor: on the morning of March 3rd, was the front desk computer patched and reporting? Most practices cannot answer that for any specific date. That gap is where the finding comes from."

**Do not** put a specific dollar penalty figure on the slide unless you have verified the current published amounts on hhs.gov that week — the tiers are adjusted annually for inflation, and a stale number in front of an office manager who checks is a credibility loss you don't recover from.

---

## Slide 2 — What Actually Goes Wrong

**Headline:** *Three failures, one root cause: nobody was watching.*

**On the slide** — three short scenarios, no vendor names:

1. **The endpoint that went dark.** A workstation stops reporting in October. Nobody notices until January, when it turns out its antivirus had been disabled since a failed update. Four months of unmonitored exposure on a machine that displays charts all day.
2. **The offboarding that never finished.** A hygienist leaves in June. Their credentials still work in September. There is no record of what was accessed in between, because nothing logs it.
3. **The ransomware Monday.** Encrypted at 6 a.m. The practice cannot tell OCR which records were touched, so the breach notification is scoped to *every* patient rather than the actual few.

**What you say**

"Notice none of these is a hacking story. They're all visibility stories. In every one, the practice's real problem wasn't the incident — it was being unable to say what happened, to whom, and when."

**Transition:** "So let's talk about what 'being able to answer' actually costs."

## Slide 3 — Continuous Visibility, Documented

**Headline:** *Every endpoint. Every day. On the record.*

**On the slide** — the offer as *outcomes*, not features:

| What you get | Why it matters at audit |
|---|---|
| Every workstation enrolled and inventoried | You can produce a device list on demand |
| Reporting status tracked continuously | You can show coverage for any date in the period |
| Silent endpoints surfaced and escalated | A machine that goes dark is a ticket, not a discovery six months later |
| Per-practice isolation | Your data is scoped to your practice, not pooled in a shared view |
| Monthly written monitoring report | A dated artifact for your compliance file |

**One live screenshot** of the dashboard fleet view. One. Not a tour.

**What you say**

"This is the part that changes your audit posture. Not that we're watching — that we're writing it down, every month, in a form you can hand to someone."

> **Sales discipline:** describe your coverage hours honestly. If you are not staffing overnight escalation, say "monitored continuously, with escalation during business hours" — not "24/7." An office manager who later learns the difference will remember that you told them, or that you didn't.

## Slide 4 — Compliance Automation: Your Evidence File, Built Monthly

**Headline:** *The paperwork is the product.*

**On the slide**

- **Signed Business Associate Agreement** — executed before we touch a single machine, not after
- **Monthly Endpoint Monitoring Report** — dated, scoped, filed
- **Device inventory** — current, exportable
- **Named accountability** — every action taken by an identified technician, not a shared login
- **Annual security risk assessment support** — the documentation OCR asks for first

**The reframe to land**

"Most practices treat compliance as an annual scramble. What we're describing is a file that builds itself twelve times a year. When the assessment comes, you're not reconstructing — you're handing over what's already there."

**Anticipate the objection:** *"Our IT company already does this."*
Response: "They might. Two questions — do you have a signed BAA with them on file right now, and can they produce a dated report for last March? If both answers are yes, you're in good shape and I'll say so."

That answer costs you nothing and buys enormous credibility, because most of the time at least one answer is no.

## Slide 5 — What This Costs, and What It Replaces

**Headline:** *Priced against the exposure, not against antivirus.*

**On the slide**

- Tiered by enrolled endpoints and locations — [insert your tiers]
- What it replaces: fragmented per-machine tools, the annual documentation scramble, and the reliance on someone remembering to check
- What it does not replace: your practice's own policies, training, and internal safeguards — we support those, we don't substitute for them
- Cyber liability carriers increasingly ask whether endpoint monitoring is in place; being able to answer yes with documentation is often a rate conversation

**The close — ask for the assessment, not the sale**

"I'd like to run a no-cost endpoint risk assessment. We inventory every machine, check what's reporting and what isn't, and give you a written summary. If it comes back clean, you'll have a document for your file and I'll have been useful for an afternoon. If it doesn't, you'll want to know before someone else finds it."

**Leave behind:** the assessment summary, a sample monthly monitoring report, and the BAA template for their attorney.

## Delivery Notes

**Pacing:** slides 1–2 are the problem and should run about six minutes. Slide 3 is four. Slides 4–5 are four combined. If you are still on slide 2 at minute ten, you are lecturing.

**The trap to avoid:** office managers are pitched fear constantly and have built calluses. The credibility move is the *opposite* of urgency — name a real risk, then concede plainly what you cannot do and what remains their responsibility. Conceding something is the fastest way to be believed about everything else.

**Qualify hard on slide 5.** A practice that will not sign a BAA, or that wants monitoring without the documentation layer, is not a fit at this price point. Walking away from those is what protects the margin on the ones you keep.

**Never claim a control you do not operate.** Not in a deck, not in a hallway conversation. In a regulated buy, an overstatement in the pitch becomes a misrepresentation in the contract.
